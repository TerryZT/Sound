Sound MoundResource Explanation 
The Sound Mound explanation guide was written to guide you through the phonemes that
each Sound Mound references. Within the English Language, there are a lot of words that
derive from other languages which make our language difficult to learn, especially for
young learners. The goal that I had in mind when creating this resource was to inform
educators, parents, guardians, and students on the rules, tips, tricks, and whys of our
language, all while providing a visual for students to reference. 
Sound Mounds are phonics visuals that allow students to see where certain graphemes are
located within the beginning, middle, and ending of words based on the placement of their
sound. 
This resource is organized by category of graphemes. Each page is labeled with the
phoneme that is being targeted. You will find the graphemes correspond to the
differentiated Sound Mounds. There are word lists under each phoneme for each
grapheme that is being practiced. You can use these guides for dictation activities,
phonemic awareness activities, or for decoding activities for students.
If there is a rule that pertains to that certain grapheme-phoneme correspondence,  it is
written within this resource. It is important to understand that not all graphemes have a
rule that can be applied when teaching students the targeted skill.
When using the Sound Mounds, it is important to have students apply sound mapping of
the sounds that students hear within words. This in turn will promote the skill of
orthographic mapping, which is a mental process that CANNOT be taugth to a child. The
process of mapping words helps students to permanently retain certain spellings of
words. 
It is also important to practice these skills by decoding them within text. Click HERE for
the link to the decodable sentences to promote fluency! These align with the Sound
Mounds. ©TheEppichClassroom

Sound Mounds are phonics visuals that help students see where we hear certain
graphemes within the beginning, middle, and ending parts of words or syllables.
What are phonemes?
Phonemes are units of sounds that we hear within words.
What are graphemes?
Graphemes are letters or combinations of letters that represent a phoneme. There can
be more than one grapheme that represents the same phoneme. 
Sound Mounds show graphemes in order of frequency within words. For example, if there
is more than one grapheme that represents the phoneme, the graphemes will go in order
from most common to least common per position of the grapheme (beginning, middle,
end). See the example below with the long o. There are more than one way that each
phoneme is represented within middle and ending parts, and they are in order based on
frequency of the graphemes. 
 
Sound Mounds are differentiated to match the progression of phonics skill that would be
taught to students starting in Kindergarten and increasing by grade level. The Sound
Mounds are easy to differentiate and are helpful to students who are not ready to
progress to grade level skills yet.
It is important to keep in mind that Sound Mounds represent words and syllables. When
using the Sound Mounds, words with two or more syllables can also be encoded using the
visual. It is always important to count syllables when starting the process of encoding a
word. Even if the word is one syllable, it is important to have students become conditioned
to counting syllables to produce fluent readers and writers.

What does the symbol mean?
On certain Sound Mound cards, you will see a heart or a star. These symbols are visuals to
help students with specific patterns.
The heart       is a visual to help students with words that contain a heart part. A word
with a heart part can be a grapheme that has not been introduced yet or a trick
grapheme that is irregular. The heart part of a word can be modeled by first mapping
out the phonemes of the word, and then heart the trick part of the word with a heart
above grapheme. For example, the word said is a trick word where the ai makes the short
E sound. This Sound Mound can be helpful for teaching this trick word.

The star       symbol reprents the patterns that are used in multi-syllable words. You will
see this symbol most frequently above open syllable vowels. You can read more about each
open syllable in the long vowel section. The graphemes that represents open syllables are
typically the most frequent grapheme representing the long vowel in our language in words
with more than one syllable. When you are writing words with more than one syllable and
that syllable ends with a long vowel, it will more than likely be an open syllable vowel.  
For example, the word baby is broken up into two syllables. The syllables are ba/by. When
mapping out the word, the star shows students since there is more than one syllable in the
word, that is the most common way we see the long a represented within a multi-syllable
word.
Beginning Sounds:
When teaching with the Sound Mounds, there will be times when there is not beginning sound
shown to represent a grapheme. If we look at the /ou/ Sound Mound, we see the ou in the
middle of the Sound Mound. 
If we look at the word ouch, we hear the /ou/ at the beginning of the word. Since there is
not beginning sound, you would shift to the next sound that you would see on the Sound
Mound- this /ou/ would be represented by the ou grapheme. This would only change if the
/l/ or /n/ is heard at the end of the word. As always, it is important to map the words and
talk about correct spelling. 
*Keep in mind Sound Mounds are NOT a one size fits all aid, but instead, are for where we
typically hear sounds within words.*

Short E 
Tips
There are no particular tips when teaching the ea grapheme. The
variation of ea comes from the evolution of the English language through
the Great Vowel Shift. In Middle English, the ea represented the long e
sound to be able to differentiate homophones that contained e-e making
the long e sound. Now, it stands as an irregular grapheme producing the
short e sound. 
You will notice a lot of the words that contain ea end with /d/ or /th/. 
The best way to help learn these words is for students to map out the
sounds within the words and then map the word. On the Sound Mound
cards, there is a heart showing the short ea being an irregular phoneme.
Students can heart this part to help with their sound mapping. 
Also, The short E can sometimes be mistaken for the short I sound.
Mouth articulation is important when teaching these sounds. You can use
this rhyme to help: /e/ makes me drop my chin, /i/ makes me grin. 
Also, the word said can be a trick word within the short e. The ai in said
used to be the pronounced with the long a sound, but when the vowel
transition happened it was not carried over. The word said can be taught
as a heart part. The Sound Mound references this as well.

Schwa
Tips
* The schwa is an unstressed syllable that makes a neutral
vowel sound (or the lazy vowel sound).
* It is the MOST common vowel in the English language.
* When a is at the end of a word or syllable, it will produce the
schwa sound.  
* Y can also make the schwa sound in certain occasions. It is
not common, but can happen within some words. (ex.
Pennsylvania, syringe, vinyl, etc.) 
* It is incredibly important to map out sounds within words to
identify the schwa sound. The schwa will explain the irregular
sounds that are made within words that do not match the
normal short vowel sound. HOWEVER, it is important to teach
students that the schwa IS a regular, and important, vowel
sound in our language.

Tips
* With the wh digraph, there are a few generalizations that can be
followed when teaching how to differentiate grapheme from the w.
These categories fall under the following: 
Short Vowels go with W:
If a short vowel is found after the /w/ phoneme, the w grapheme
will be used. (web, want, wick, went)
Words with Silent-E:
If a word contains the silent-e at the ending, the digraph wh will be
used. (whale, white, while)
Question Words:
If the word is a question word, it will begin with the digraph wh.
(who, what, when, where, why)
Action/Sound Words:
If the word is an action or a sound word, the digraph why will be
used. (whisk, whoosh, whistle)
* When looking at words that begin with the letter w, often times
the grapheme a that follows will produce the schwa sound. This is due
to the changing of the sound throughout the history of the English
language. The /w/ phoneme does influence mouth articulation too, so
these can be taught as heart parts of words. (want, watch, wash)

/ch/
©TheEppichClassroom
Tips
* The phoneme /ch/ can be found at both the beginning and ending of
words or syllables. When teaching the progression of this phonemes, the
graphemes that go along with each of the graphemes that represent /ch/
should be considered.
When /ch/ comes at the beginning of a word, the most common
graphemes that follow are long vowel teams or r-controlled vowels. This
can be difficult for beginning readers who have not been introduced to
these sounds yet. There are some words that do start with the ch
grapheme and have short vowels follow like chip, chess, and chick. 
When /ch/ comes at the ending of a word, the same concept follows as
discussed above. We use the ch at the end of a word when there is a long
vowel team, diphthong, or r-controlled vowel that comes before the /ch/.
Examples of these words include peach and porch. However, if there is a
consonant before the /ch/ as in punch, the ch grapheme will be used. 
Another /ch/ ending is represented by the -tch grapheme. This only comes
at the ending of words or syllables. The -tch is used when there is a short
vowel before the /ch/ sound. Examples of these words include stitch,
swatch, and watch.
I think it is important to teach students the -tch ending early in their
writing, because this is an important rule to know.

-s or -es
©TheEppichClassroom
Tips
* The suffix s can make three sounds: /s/, /z/, or /ez/. This
depends on the phoneme that the base word of the suffix ends
with. 
The -s makes the /s/ phoneme when there is a voiceless consonant
that comes before the suffix. These letters are ones in which we
do not feel a vibration in the throat when the sound is produced.
These letters include p, t, c, k, q, f, h, s, and x. However, if a base
word ends in s or x, the suffix will produces the /iz/ sound.
The -s suffix makes the /z/ phoneme when there is a voiced
consonant or a vowel at the end of the base word. The voiced
consonants included b, d, g, j, l, m, n, ng, r, z, th (voiced), v, w, y,
and z. These sounds can be felt on the throat when the sound is
produced.
The -es suffix makes the /iz/ phoneme when the noun ends in the
following consonants: -s, -x, -z, -sh, -ch. The suffix that will be
used is -es with these words. This will produce the /iz/ sound. 
Sometimes the addition of a suffix can add a singular sound, but
sometimes it makes the word gain a syllable. Talk through these
words as you begin to create using the suffix.
